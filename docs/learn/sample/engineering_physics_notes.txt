--- Page 1: Dynamics and Newton's Laws of Motion ---
Newton's First Law of Motion states that an object remains at rest or in uniform rectilinear motion unless acted upon by a non-zero resultant net external force. An inertial frame of reference is one in which Newton's first law holds true without the introduction of fictitious inertial forces.

Newton's Second Law of Motion establishes the quantitative link between dynamics and kinematics: the net force acting on a body is equal to the time rate of change of its linear momentum. For a constant mass system, this reduces to the fundamental vector equation F_net = m*a. Consequently, acceleration is directly proportional to the applied net force and inversely proportional to the mass of the body. If the net force acting on a constant mass is doubled, the resulting acceleration is doubled instantaneously.

Newton's Third Law states that whenever body A exerts a force on body B, body B simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force on body A (F_AB = -F_BA). These two forces act on different bodies and therefore never cancel each other out in single-body equilibrium calculations.

--- Page 2: Work and the Work-Energy Theorem ---
Work done by a constant force F over a displacement d is defined as the scalar dot product W = F * d * cos(theta), where theta is the angle between the force and displacement vectors. Kinetic friction acts opposite to the direction of relative displacement; hence the work done by kinetic friction is always negative, dissipating mechanical energy into thermal energy.

The Work-Energy Theorem states that the net work done by all forces (both conservative and non-conservative) on a particle equals the net change in the particle's kinetic energy: W_net = Delta(KE) = (1/2)*m*v_final^2 - (1/2)*m*v_initial^2. If net work is positive, kinetic energy increases; if net work is negative, kinetic energy decreases.

--- Page 3: Simple Harmonic Motion ---
A simple harmonic oscillator obeys Hooke's Law: the restoring force is directly proportional to displacement from equilibrium and directed opposite to the displacement: F = -k*x.
The equation of motion is given by m*(d^2x/dt^2) + k*x = 0, yielding angular frequency omega = sqrt(k/m) and period T = 2*pi*sqrt(m/k).
In simple harmonic motion, total mechanical energy is conserved: E_total = (1/2)*k*A^2 = (1/2)*m*v^2 + (1/2)*k*x^2.
At the extreme displacement points (x = +/- A), velocity is zero and acceleration is at its maximum magnitude. At the equilibrium midpoint (x = 0), acceleration is zero and velocity is at its maximum magnitude.
